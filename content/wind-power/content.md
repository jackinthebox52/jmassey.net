# The Economics of Iowa's Wind Turbines: A Deep Dive

I was recently driving across my home state of Iowa on I-80, and I was enraptured by the scale and quantity of the wind turbines I've seen many times before. I became curious about the financial vialbility of wind power, due to the relatively low cost of electricity and the massive construction costs that come with mounting a trubine hundreds of feet in the air. This can't make money, right? But, MidAmercian and others do deploy them with their own captial, so my instinct must be wrong, right? Well, I stayed up all night to figure this out. How many are there? Who owns them? Who bears the cost or reaps the benefits? And most importantly, do they actually make economic sense?

## The Scale of Iowa's Wind Power

Iowa has over 6,000 wind turbines generating more than 12,200 megawatts of capacity, making it second in the nation for turbine count. Wind power produces over 62% of Iowa's electricity. MidAmerican Energy dominates the region, operating about 3,400 turbines (56% of the state total) across 34 counties. Their 2015 expansion of 448 Siemens turbines costing $1.9 billion provides a good case study for understanding wind power.

## How Much Power Do They Actually Produce?

A 2.3 MW turbine doesn't run at full capacity all the time. The "capacity factor" (actual output versus maximum possible) averages 33.3% in Iowa, though newer turbines are hitting 38.2%. This means a 2.3 MW turbine produces about 6,709 MWh annually rather than the theoretical 20,148 MWh. Over a 25-year lifespan, that's 167,725 MWh per turbine—enough to power about 626 average American homes for a quarter century.

Why the improvement in newer turbines? Bigger rotors, taller towers, and better engineering. Modern turbines have rotors over 400 feet in diameter (longer than a football field) and reach heights of nearly 600 feet to capture stronger, more consistent winds. The real innovation is "specific power"—the ratio of generator size to rotor swept area. By putting oversized rotors on relatively smaller generators, newer turbines can generate power even in moderate winds rather than waiting for strong gusts. MidAmerican's oldest turbines were 233 feet tall; their newest are nearly 600 feet. In 2023, 98% of newly installed turbines featured rotors 115 meters or larger, compared to zero turbines of that size in 2013. Hub heights have increased 83% since 1998. This isn't just incremental improvement—it's a fundamental redesign of how wind turbines capture energy.

## The Problem of "Wasted" Wind

Here's something that surprised me: about 4.7% of Iowa's wind power gets curtailed—meaning turbines are shut down even when the wind is blowing because the grid can't handle the excess power. For our 448-turbine project, that's roughly 141,000 MWh lost annually, or about $9.3 million in revenue that just disappears. Wind farms don't get compensated for curtailment; they simply lose both the electricity sales and their production tax credits. In 2023, MISO (Iowa's grid operator) curtailed an average of 508 MW every hour. Texas solved similar issues with a $7 billion grid upgrade that reduced curtailment from 17% to 0.5%.

## The Lifetime Costs Add Up

Over a 25-year lifespan, the math looks like this: the initial $4.24 million per turbine adds another $2.88 million in operations and maintenance costs. That's $7.12 million total per turbine, or $3.19 billion for the entire 448-turbine project. Annual O&M costs start around $115,000 per turbine and increase as components wear out. Between years 10-15, O&M costs jump from $45,000/MW to $60,000/MW as major components need replacement.

The O&M breakdown reveals where the money goes: turbine maintenance itself (scheduled and unscheduled) averages $20/kW-yr, while everything else—property taxes, insurance, land lease payments, administration, and grid connection fees—adds another $25/kW-yr. Unscheduled maintenance accounts for 30-60% of total O&M costs, with gearbox failures being particularly expensive. Over the turbine's life, cumulative O&M costs reach 65-95% of the initial investment. Still, that's remarkably cheap compared to fossil fuel plants that require constant fuel purchases and have their own maintenance nightmares.

## How They're Actually Profitable

Revenue comes from two sources: selling electricity at wholesale rates (~$40/MWh) and the federal Production Tax Credit (2.6¢/kWh for the first 10 years). For our project: $3.0 billion in electricity sales plus $780 million in PTCs over 10 years, minus $178 million in curtailment losses, minus $3.19 billion in costs equals about $410 million profit over 25 years. That's a 21.6% return on investment over 25 years—barely over 0.8% annually. Without the PTC, these projects would barely break even. The tax credit is absolutely essential to making wind economically viable.

The PTC creates some perverse incentives. Wind farms will sometimes generate electricity even when wholesale prices go negative—actually paying to produce power—because the 2.6¢/kWh tax credit is worth more than the negative price penalty. This has led to situations where coal plants crowd out cheaper wind by operating at losses, costing MISO customers $1.1 billion between 2021-2023. The broader challenge is that wind's zero marginal cost (no fuel to buy) means it undercuts all other generation sources, but the intermittency and curtailment mean it can't be the sole solution. The economics only work with patient capital—MidAmerican's Berkshire Hathaway ownership provides exactly that, along with massive tax liability from other businesses that the PTCs can offset.

## Who Pays and Who Benefits?

Here's the critical question: Iowa households pay federal taxes that fund wind PTCs. Does this just shuffle costs around? The math is clear: the average Iowa household pays about $23 annually toward the $3.4 billion national PTC program. Meanwhile, Iowa's electricity rates are 39% below the national average—saving the typical household $173+ per year. That's a net benefit of $150 annually. Why? Iowa has 13× more wind capacity per capita than it pays for in taxes. Essentially, the entire country is subsidizing infrastructure that keeps Iowa's electricity cheap permanently. MidAmerican customers have the 5th lowest rates among investor-owned utilities nationally, and the company has credited over $900 million back to customers since 1997 thanks to wind generation.

## The Broader Picture

The wind industry has created a genuine economic advantage for Iowa. Businesses benefit even more from low industrial rates (5.3¢/kWh), Iowa exports excess electricity to neighboring states, and farmers receive thousands in annual royalties for hosting turbines. Electric cooperatives alone pay $29 million in local and state taxes yearly. The technology keeps improving—capacity factors climbing from 33% to 38%+ means more power from the same investment. Battery storage is finally arriving at scale with projects like Alliant's planned 280 MW facility, which could capture curtailed wind for later use.

## Energy Storage: The Missing Piece

Iowa's battery storage buildout is still modest but growing rapidly. Alliant Energy currently operates four battery facilities ranging from 250 kW to 5 MW, with that much larger 280 MW facility planned for Lansing by 2026. NextEra has a 75 MW/300 MWh battery project at the former Duane Arnold nuclear plant site. MidAmerican runs a 1 MW pilot facility in Knoxville. National Grid Renewables got approval for a 100 MW solar project paired with a 50 MW battery system in Greene County. These projects could transform the curtailment problem—instead of shutting down turbines when the grid is full, excess wind power could charge batteries for discharge during peak demand hours when wholesale prices are highest. Iowa State University is building the state's first rural microgrid in Montezuma with solar panels and batteries that could cut energy costs 18%. Still, total battery capacity remains tiny compared to the 12,200 MW of wind capacity and 508 MW average hourly curtailment.

## The Future: Can Wind Get 50% Cheaper?

Here's where things get exciting. A 2021 Nature Energy survey of 163 global wind experts predicts that wind energy costs could drop 37-49% by 2050, with a 10% chance of reductions exceeding 50%. For land-based wind specifically, experts anticipate 24% cost reductions by 2030 and 35% by 2050 under median scenarios. The aggressive scenario projects cost reductions up to 40% by 2030 alone.

What drives these improvements? The same factors that got us from 33% to 38% capacity factors, but taken further. By 2030, average North American onshore turbines are expected to reach 3.25 MW with 115-meter hub heights—evolutionary growth that continues the trend. Offshore turbines will see revolutionary growth to 11 MW average by 2030. NREL's research shows that technology innovations like on-site manufacturing, taller towers, longer segmented blades, and "wake steering" (using controls to redirect turbines and avoid impacting downstream units) could unlock an additional 80% economically viable wind capacity by 2025.

The improvements aren't just theoretical. LCOE (levelized cost of energy) for onshore wind has already fallen from $5,326/kW in 1983 to $1,694/kW in 2023—a 71% decline. Wind project costs have dropped 58% since 2012. A recent MIT study found that software-only optimization of turbine blade angles at existing wind farms can increase output by 1.2% overall and 3% during optimal wind conditions—equivalent to adding 3,600 turbines globally at zero capital cost. Advanced materials like carbon fiber composites enable longer, lighter blades. Multi-rotor designs (like Vestas' four-rotor turbine) distribute mechanical loads more efficiently. Better aerodynamics inspired by computational modeling harvest more energy at lower wind speeds.

The capacity factor improvements compound over decades. If Iowa's fleet moves from 33.3% to 42% capacity factor (well within projected improvements), each turbine would produce an additional 1,500 MWh annually—worth $60,000 per turbine per year. Over 25 years, that's $1.5 million in additional revenue per turbine without building anything new. For the 448-turbine project, that would add $672 million in lifetime revenue. Combined with cost reductions, future wind projects could achieve the same output as today's projects for half the investment, or double the output for the same investment.

The evidence suggests continued investment and runway for the wind sector will deliver substantial returns. Each generation of turbines significantly outperforms the last, and the pace of improvement shows no signs of slowing. The question isn't whether wind will get cheaper—it's whether we'll invest enough to capture those gains.

## The Bottom Line

Wind turbines in Iowa work economically, but it's a delicate balance. Federal tax policy is essential—without the PTC, investment would drop by two-thirds. Transmission constraints waste nearly 5% of generated power, costing hundreds of millions annually. Yet Iowa has built a system where residents pay $23 in federal taxes and save $173 on their electric bills. That's not just moving money around—that's genuinely capturing value through geographic arbitrage and zero-fuel-cost generation. The turbines I saw driving across Iowa represent one of the most successful regional energy transformations in the country, even if the profit margins are thinner than you might expect.`
